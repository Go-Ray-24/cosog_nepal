Built for Nepal's fire season

The Chure and Terai forests accumulate dry leaf-litter and grass fuel through the pre-monsoon window (Feb–May) — when the vast majority of FIRMS detections cluster. FireWatch is designed to shorten the gap between a satellite overpass and boots on the ground.

  • VIIRS 375 m resolution catches smaller, cooler fires than MODIS
  • Confidence & FRP filtering suppresses agricultural-burn false positives
  • Registered buffer-zone committees and park stations as alert recipients

Understanding the data

Every point on the map comes from a weather satellite that measures heat, not smoke or flames. Here's what the numbers mean in plain terms.

🛰️ What is NASA FIRMS?

FIRMS (Fire Information for Resource Management System) is a free NASA service that publishes the locations of active fires detected by satellites, usually within 1–3 hours of the satellite passing overhead. It does not predict fires — it reports heat that is already burning.

📡 VIIRS vs. MODIS — which sensor?

Both are instruments on NASA satellites that scan the whole Earth roughly twice a day.

  • VIIRS (375 m): newer, sharper. Each detection covers an area about 375 m across, so it catches smaller and cooler fires. Preferred for early warning.
  • MODIS (1 km): older, coarser. Good for large fires and long historical records, but misses small ones.

The number in brackets is the pixel size — the smallest patch of ground the sensor can resolve.

🔥 FRP — Fire Radiative Power

FRP is how much heat energy the fire is giving off at the moment of the satellite pass, measured in megawatts (MW). Think of it as the fire's "brightness".

  • Low (< 10 MW): small grass or leaf-litter fire, or a controlled agricultural burn.
  • Moderate (10–50 MW): an established fire spreading through undergrowth.
  • High (> 50 MW): intense crown fire or a large burn front.

On the map, higher FRP makes a point bigger and pushes the heatmap toward red.

🎯 Confidence — how sure is the satellite?

An estimate of how likely the detection is a real fire rather than sun-glint, hot bare soil, or an industrial heat source.

  • High — almost certainly a real fire.
  • Nominal — probably real; treat as worth checking.
  • Low — weak signal; could be a false alarm.
🕒 Acquisition time & Day / Night

Acq time is the moment (in UTC) the satellite recorded the heat — not when the fire started. Day / Night tells you whether the pass was in daylight; night passes detect fires more reliably because there's no solar interference.

📍 Geo-fence & nearest-authority alert

A geo-fence is a boundary you draw on the live map around a place you care about — a community forest, a village, a park. If any detection falls inside it, the dashboard flags a breach and can email the closest registered contact, calculated as the straight-line (haversine) distance from the fire to every registered location.

⚠️ Satellite detections are an aid, not a guarantee. A clear sky is required for a detection, passes happen only a few times a day, and small or early fires can be missed. Always confirm with people on the ground.
